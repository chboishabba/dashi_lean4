			<H1>
BOOK: TOPOLOGY and GROUPOIDS <br />
 by Ronald Brown
</H1>

 <div class="one-half">
<p class="italic bold" style="font-size:80%"> "......people
      are accustomed to work with fundamental groups and generators and relations
      for these and stick to it, even in contexts when this is wholly inadequate,
      namely when you get a clear description by generators and relations only
      when working simultaneously with a whole bunch of base-points chosen with
      care - or equivalently working in the algebraic context of <I>groupoids</I>,
      rather than groups. Choosing paths for connecting the base points natural
      to the situation to one among them, and reducing the groupoid to a single
      &nbsp;group, will then hopelessly destroy the structure and inner symmetries
      of the situation, and result in a mess of generators and relations no one
      dares to write down, because everyone feels they won't be of any use whatever,
      and just confuse the picture rather than clarify it. I have known such perplexity
      myself a long time ago, namely in Van Kampen type situations, whose only
      understandable formulation is in terms of (amalgamated sums of)
      groupoids."</p>

			
			</div>

          <div class="one-half last">
      <h4>  Alexander Grothendieck</h4>

	<p>(for more of his comments on mathematics <A HREF="pstacks.html">click here</A>)
   </p>

 <b>Excellent perspective on topology</b><br>
 <i>Staffan Angere, Lund University</i><p>

This is an introductory book on topology, with a focus on homotopy theory. It is written from a 
largely category-theoretical viewpoint. However, unlike other such treatments, 
such as May's "Concise course", it always keeps geometric intuition close at hand.
 This makes the category theory come very naturally, and also makes the book easier 
 to understand for the non-category theorist. Since it also has very few prerequisites 
 (e.g. no previous knowledge of topology or category theory is required), 
 it would work very well for a first undergraduate course on topology. However, 
 because of its insightful and slightly uncommon take on topology as a whole, I would 
 guess that even some working mathematicians might learn a thing or two, or find new things 
 to think about. The book as a whole indicates very well how a groupoidal way of doing 
 one-dimensional homotopy theory is far more natural than the standard group-theoretical one. 
 Now all we need is an equally natural and accessible extension to the higher homotopy groups.<br>
<i>amazon.com review:, February 11, 2013 </i>

<p>
Here is a link to a
<A HREF="http://poncelet.sciences.univ-metz.fr/~tu/IHP/overview.html">review
of groupoids in the context of stacks in geometry and physics</A>.
<P>
<A HREF="pdffiles/groupoidsurvey.pdf">From groups to groupoids: a brief survey
</A>&nbsp;Bull LMS 19 (1987) 113-134. &nbsp; &nbsp;Link to pdf file.
<P>



<P>
This is a retitled, &nbsp;revised, updated and extended edition of a classic
text, first published in 1968. (Sample Chapter as pdf file:
<A HREF="pdffiles/topgpds-ch9.pdf">9 Computation of the fundamental groupoid</A>).
<P>
<A HREF="newedition.html">The new edition</A>
<P>
Its first half gives a geometric account of general topology appropriate
to a beginning course in algebraic topology. For example, it includes
identification spaces, adjunction spaces and finite cell complexes, and a
convenient category of spaces.
<P>
The second half introduces the algebra of
<A href="gpdsweb.html">groupoids</A> and shows the utility of this algebra
for modelling geometry. It is also one of the few basic topology texts to
emphasise the importance
of<A href="http://plato.stanford.edu/entries/category-theory/"> categorical
methods</A> and universal properties in allowing
<A href="pdffiles/Analogy-and-Comparison.pdf">analogies</A> between different mathematical structures. Thus the
notion of pushout is used to describe (i) ways of constructing topological
spaces from basic examples, and (ii) how a variety of modelling and of
calculations follows from the way the#0000ff>fundamental groupoid
on a set of base points preserves certain pushouts of spaces. Some
of the proofs of results on the fundamental groupoid would be difficult to
envisage except in the form given:#8000ff>`We verify the required
universal property'. An example is the main result on
<A HREF="pdffiles/rbpjh-orbit0212271v1.pdf">orbit spaces and orbit groupoids
</A>in Chapter 11 (this is published nowhere else).
<P>
The overall aim was to give the best context for the required theorems, so
that they seem natural, simpler to prove and in the strongest form; in other
words, to understand the mathematics! &nbsp;The resulting exposition is non
standard, and has been described as `idiosyncratic'! So be it!
<P>
Here are some examples of topics covered not available in other texts at
this level:
<UL>

   <li style="list-style-type:disc;"> the <b>initial </b> topology on joins of spaces;</li>

   <li style="list-style-type:disc;"> the convenient category of <B>k</B>-spaces,  in the non Hausdorff case;</li>
<li style="list-style-type:disc;">
    a <b>gluing theorem</b>  for homotopy equivalences (this result has now become a standard lemma in abstract homotopy theory, but first appeared in the 1968 edition of this book; 
		the proof here, which generalises the standard result that a homotopy equivalence of spaces induces isomorphisms of the (pointed, of course) homotopy groups,  has the advantage of giving control of the homotopies involved);
		<LI> the notion of a path in a space <i>X</i> as a map from [0,<i>r</i>] to <i>X</i>, for <i>r</i> &ge; 0, gives a <b>category of paths</b>. This  
		 makes the work easier for students, 	since associativity comes for free, 	and also 
		shows a purely  algebraic use of categories: (this approach is also taken in the book on "Knot Theory" by Crowell and Fox);
		<LI> this book is the <b>only</b> topology text in English to prove a version, dating from 1967,  of the van Kampen Theorem for the fundamental groupoid
		 on a <b>set of base points</b> ,
		 and so deduce the fundamental group of the circle - for more discussion on many base points see 
		 <a href="http://mathoverflow.net/questions/40945/compelling-evidence-that-two-basepoints-are-better-than-one">this mathoverflow discussion </a>; 
</li>
		<li style="list-style-type:disc;"> the
   Jordan
    Curve theorem, proved via the  <A href="pdffiles/brouwer-cor-fin.pdf">Phragmen-Brouwer property</A>, and as a consequence
    of the groupoid (many base point) van Kampen theorem;
	</li>
<li style="list-style-type:disc;">
    the equivalence between the categories of covering maps of spaces and covering
    morphisms of groupoids  (this is surely the right base point free approach to covering space  theory, since a <b>covering map</b>
		 is modelled by a <b>covering morphism</b>, which makes it easier to deal with lifting problems for maps);
  </li>

  <li style="list-style-type:disc;">  the fundamental groupoid of an orbit space by a discontinuous action of a
    group as the <b>orbit groupoid </b> of the fundamental groupoid (this
    is a powerful result not generally recognised). Ross Geoghegan in his 1986 review (MR0760769) of two papers by M.A. Armstrong on the fundamental groups of orbit spaces 
wrote: "These two papers show which parts of elementary covering space theory carry over from the free to the nonfree case. 
This is the kind of basic material that ought to have been in standard textbooks on fundamental groups for the last fifty years." At present, 
to my knowledge,  "Topology and Groupoids" 
is the only topology text to treat such results. 
</li>
</UL>
<P>
I hope that the availability of this text will help further investigation
of related topics. For example: can these orbit groupoid methods be applied to areas such
as braid groups and mapping class groups? Also, this text should be a useful
foundation for those wishing to study the applications of <A HREF="hdaweb2.html">
higher homotopy groupoids</A> to <A HREF="nonab-a-t.html">
Nonabelian algebraic topology</A> . This is quite an open field with not much
competition in algebraic topology, as the workers there hardly use groupoids
at all. However groupoids are widely used in noncommutative geometry, certain
areas of differential topology and the theory of stacks and gerbes.
<P>

<P>
Also included: Prefaces; Appendix on set theory and cardinality; Glossary
of terms from set theory; Glossary of symbols. The bibliography, notes and
other discussions are intended to put the work in context.
